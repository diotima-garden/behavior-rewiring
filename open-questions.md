# Open Questions

What is genuinely unresolved, and where the seed material overstated its case. Kept honest
so this node doesn't calcify into confident folklore. An entry leaves this file when it
gets an answer in `sources.md` — or when it is settled as unanswerable and recorded as a
bet.

---

## 1. How many active rewires at once? — the number is folklore

The seed document answered this with a confident table: roughly 85% success with one
habit, ~35% with two, under 5% with three or more ("systemic collapse"). **No such study
is traceable.** It is not in the habit-formation literature, and its stated mechanism —
willpower depletion — is the one claim in this area that definitively failed to replicate
(`sources.md` §1). Treat those figures as invented.

What survives is directional, not numeric: splitting effort across simultaneous targets
degrades each, and vague vigilance over many triggers is not the primed state that makes
interception work. `principles.md` §2 therefore rules **one active target** on a detection
argument and declines to attach a percentage to it.

**Still open:** whether the real active limit is one or two, and whether it varies with how
distinguishable the cues are. Two targets with sharply distinct, non-overlapping cues may
be closer to two independent sprints than to a split-attention problem — the detection
model predicts this, and nothing here tests it. **The grove's own catch-logs are the only
data we will ever have on it.** If a grove ever runs two cleanly separated targets and logs
both, that is worth recording here.

**First field observation (2026-10, uninstrumented).** A consumer practising interception
in one interpersonal-pressure context reports improved detection in a second such context
without any training targeted at the second. That is what `principles.md` §3 predicts when
the two situations share a cue class — a line is crossed and accommodation is expected —
and it is *not* evidence for a strengthened general faculty, which `sources.md` §4 rules
out. The distinction matters because it is testable in opposite directions: a shared cue
predicts transfer only to contexts carrying that cue, a general faculty predicts transfer
anywhere. One self-reported case with no log behind it, recorded so the next one can be
compared against it. **Relatedly open:** whether cue-class spillover costs any detection
budget at all, or whether it is free.

## 2. Does a text simulator transfer at all?

The mechanism (rehearsal → graded exposure → real reps) is well evidenced; LLM-graded text
chat is the weakest point on that spectrum and is unvalidated (`sources.md` §5). The
specific unknown is not whether it builds recognition — it plausibly does — but whether
recognition built at zero arousal survives contact with real arousal, which is exactly the
acquisition/performance boundary (`principles.md` §5).

Worse, the grader *is* the feedback mechanism, and its validity is unestablished. A
miscalibrated grader trains a confident wrong reflex, which is strictly worse than no
drill. The consuming grove has this as its top-priority item; until it is addressed, every
claim downstream of the simulator is provisional.

## 3. Is embodied practice actually faster?

The bet: physical disciplines under load (sparring, cold, hard training) rewire regulation
faster than verbal reasoning does, because the body cannot intellectualize its way out and
the arousal is real. Consistent with the stress-inoculation evidence; the *comparison* to
verbal/cognitive practice is untested.

Also unresolved and more interesting: **does regulation trained under physical arousal
transfer to social arousal?** The seed material asserted it does — same pathways. Given how
badly general inhibition training transfers (`sources.md` §4), the prior should be
pessimistic: expect it to be another domain-specific skill unless shown otherwise. If true
it would be a large shortcut, which is reason for suspicion, not enthusiasm.

## 4. Where is the line between managed and rewired?

A delay rule can make someone's behavior indistinguishable from someone who no longer has
the impulse. From outside, and sometimes from inside, the two look the same — effortful
compliance versus an absent urge. The conventional markers (effort felt, residual tension,
slips under load) are introspective and unreliable in exactly the direction that flatters
the practitioner.

This matters practically: a grove that declares victory on behavioral compliance will stop
too early, and one that demands a felt absence of the urge may never let anything be
banked. `protocols/single-target-sprint.md` §6 currently rules on *automaticity at low
load*, which is a compromise rather than an answer.

**Partial answer, graded in (2026-10): the binary is probably false.** `sources.md` §11 —
the inhibitory-learning account of extinction (**A-**) holds that the original association
is never erased; a competing association is learned and outcompetes it, which is why
spontaneous recovery, context renewal, reinstatement and rapid reacquisition all exist.
If that is the shape of the mechanism, "managed versus rewired" is not a line to locate.
There is one association being outcompeted by another, and which one wins is
context-dependent. Practical consequences: a response returning after a context change is
the model's prediction rather than evidence the work failed, and the absence of a felt urge
was never the thing a sprint could promise. The caveat is domain transfer, the standing
one in this node — that corpus is fear conditioning and clinical exposure, not social cues.

**What stays open after that.** Whether the ruling in
`protocols/single-target-sprint.md` §6 should change — automaticity at low load remains a
practical stopping rule, and the inhibitory-learning picture arguably *supports* it by
removing the stricter alternative, but nothing here tests the rule itself. Also still open:
whether any introspective marker survives. The measurement problem is in fact sharpened,
not solved: both ACT and the inhibitory-learning version of exposure deliberately drop
feeling-change as the treatment goal, which is converging evidence that making the felt
state the success metric corrupts the measure — the one quantity the practitioner judges
least honestly.

## 5. What about the timeline for a whole pattern, not one behavior?

Lally's ~66-day median (range 18–254) is for **one simple daily act in a stable context**
(`sources.md` §3). The seed material extrapolated from per-behavior numbers to an 18–36
month figure for reorganizing an entire relational pattern — plausible as an order of
magnitude, but it is an extrapolation, not a measurement, and the "with high resources it
compresses to 12–24 months" refinement is pure invention.

Nothing in this node licenses a timeline for a *pattern*. Quote the per-behavior range,
note that patterns are many behaviors run sequentially, and stop there.

## 6. Does declarative support help procedural change, or just feel like it does?

An open question specific to this garden. Diotima's existing machinery is spaced
repetition — declarative, parallel, thousands of items. This node is the procedural
counterpart and scales the opposite way. The question: is there any real role for SRS here?

Candidates worth nothing: cards that restate theory (that is the epiphany high,
`principles.md` §1). Candidates maybe worth something: cards that rehearse the *cue*
("here is a situation — was a line crossed?"), which is recognition, the one thing an SRS
drill might genuinely train. That is a narrow, testable hypothesis and the only version of
"Anki for behavior change" this node currently endorses exploring.
