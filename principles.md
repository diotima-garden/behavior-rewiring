# Principles — Behavior Rewiring

The durable stance a practice grove can design against. Drill-agnostic, opinionated, and
short on mechanism: evidence grades and the places popular accounts overreach live in
`sources.md`, unresolved bets in `open-questions.md`.

The question this node exists to answer well: **how does an understood intention become
an automatic behavior, and how much of that can you run at once?**

---

## 1. Comprehension is a precondition, not a stage

Reading about a pattern changes nothing about the pattern. It supplies a map and a
dopamine hit that feels like progress — the "epiphany high" — and then the old default
fires on schedule the next morning. A grove that delivers insight and stops has delivered
nothing.

The consequence for design: **no grove should measure itself in material consumed.** The
unit of progress is a *rep* — an instance where the old response was about to fire and a
specified different one went out instead.

## 2. The scarce resource is detection, not willpower

This is the most important correction this node makes to the folk model.

The intuitive story is a willpower tank: self-control burns a finite fuel, so parallel
changes drain it and collapse. That story is empirically dead (`sources.md` §1 —
ego depletion failed two large multi-lab replications, the glucose mechanism worse).
Designing against it produces bad advice: rest your willpower, do hard things early in
the day, don't "spend" it.

The constraint that *is* real: a rewire only fires when you **notice the cue in the
moment**. Noticing is a monitoring task over a specific trigger, and monitoring many
distinct triggers at once degrades all of them — you end up vaguely vigilant rather than
specifically primed, and the specific primed state is the whole mechanism.

So budget **detection load**, not willpower. Which yields an asymmetry the willpower model
cannot see:

| Change type | Needs in-the-moment detection? | How many at once |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** — intercept an impulse and substitute a response | Yes | **One.** This is the real bottleneck. |
| **Passive** — rearrange the environment so the impulse is never cued | No | Several. They cost setup, not attention. |

A sprint is therefore *one active target plus as many passive supports as you can
arrange* — and the passive ones are underrated precisely because they feel like cheating.
They are not cheating; removing a cue is a better outcome than winning a fight with it.

## 3. Rewire a cue→response pair, never a trait

A habit is an association between a **specific recurring context** and a **specific
response**. That is what the formation literature actually describes, and it is the
sharpest practical constraint here.

- "Be less reactive" is not a target. It names no cue and no response.
- "When someone interrupts me mid-sentence, I finish the sentence at the same volume" is
  a target. It has a detectable trigger and one specified output.

Corollary: **the trainable unit is domain-specific.** The tempting move — train the
general faculty (impulse inhibition) and let it dissolve the downstream flaws — is the
"keystone habit" story, and it is the weakest-evidenced claim in this whole area
(`sources.md` §4). Lab training of general response inhibition reliably improves the
trained task and reliably fails to transfer anywhere else. Train the pause *in the
situation you want it in*.

Where keystone-like leverage is real, it is because one cue genuinely *precedes* several
behaviors — not because a muscle got stronger. If you claim leverage, name the shared
cue.

## 4. Substitute; don't suppress

"Stop doing X" leaves the cue firing with nothing to answer it, so the old response wins
by default. Every target needs a named replacement, pre-decided, available under load.

The mechanism with the best evidence in this entire node is the **if-then anchor**
(implementation intentions): specify trigger and response in advance, in concrete terms,
and the response increasingly fires on cue without a fresh decision. Effect sizes are
medium-to-large and hold up across hundreds of tests (`sources.md` §2). Nothing else here
is as well supported.

Practical form: pre-written, cue-bound, one line, and *behavioral* — a thing a body does,
not a state to achieve. "Then I will feel secure" is not an anchor. "Then I say one
sentence and stop talking" is.

## 5. Acquisition and performance are different deficits

A grove can be excellent at one and structurally unable to touch the other:

- **Acquisition** — the response isn't in the repertoire, or the crossing isn't even
  registered. Low-stakes rehearsal and simulation genuinely build this.
- **Performance** — the response exists but doesn't come out under real arousal, because
  the arousal consumes the attention that detection needs.

Simulation without stakes is a *scaffold* for the first and does little for the second.
Closing the second gap needs arousal in the loop — graded escalation, embodied/physical
practice, eventually real reps. Deliberately simulated stress is a legitimate, evidenced
route to those reps (`sources.md` §5), and the main reason to prefer it over waiting is
that real life supplies triggers on its own schedule, not yours.

This is also the honest ceiling statement a grove owes its user: *this drill trains
recognition and a pre-loaded reply; it does not inoculate you.*

## 6. Consolidation is a step, with a modest claim attached

Sleep is where a day's reps get stabilized, so protecting it belongs inside a protocol
rather than in a wellness footnote. But the defensible claim is **stabilization and
retention**, not overnight improvement, and the popular "deep sleep myelinates your new
pathway" line is a mechanism dressed up past what the evidence carries
(`sources.md` §3).

Design consequence, and it is a real one: a sprint is a *daily* structure with the nights
inside it. Reps cannot be batched into a weekend.

## 7. Timelines are months, highly variable, and not promisable

The one real number worth carrying: median time to automaticity for a simple daily
behavior in a stable context is on the order of **~2 months, with individual times
spanning weeks to most of a year** — and the targets this node cares about (interception
under social or emotional load) sit at the effortful end of that spread, not the easy end.

So: a sprint is a dose, not a cure. Thirty days is a reasonable unit of *attention*; it is
not a date by which the behavior is yours. Any grove quoting a confident timeline — or a
success percentage by number of simultaneous habits — is quoting folklore
(`open-questions.md` §1).

---

## What this implies for a child grove, in one line each

- Count reps and catches, not sessions or pages read.
- Carry exactly one active interception target at a time; log the rest as a queue.
- Make every target a cue→response pair you could write on one line.
- State your ceiling: which of acquisition/performance you actually train.
- Put the nights inside the protocol.
- Quote ranges, never dates.
