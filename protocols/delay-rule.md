# Protocol — Delay Rule

A template. Instantiate it in the child grove; the log belongs there.

**What it is for:** forcing interception on an impulse that is faster than your noticing.
A delay rule inserts a structural gap between cue and response so that the catch does not
have to be won in real time — the rule does the catching.

**Grounded in:** `principles.md` §2 (a passive support — no detection load, so it does not
consume the one active slot) and §4 (if-then anchors, the best-evidenced lever here).

---

## Form

> **When** `<cue>`, the response goes into `<a holding place>` and may not be issued for
> `<interval>`.

Three parts, all required:

1. **A cue you can bind mechanically** — pressing send, reaching for something, opening a
   specific app, being asked a question you feel pressure to answer immediately.
2. **A holding place** — somewhere the response can be fully composed and parked.
   Composing it is important: suppression leaves the impulse live, whereas a drafted,
   parked response is discharged without being emitted.
3. **A fixed, pre-committed interval.** Decided once, in advance, not negotiated at the
   moment of pressure. Minutes for fast conversational impulses; longer for anything
   written.

## Why it works without willpower

The impulse is not resisted — it is *rerouted*. The energy goes into drafting, the
interval does the inhibiting, and by the time the interval expires the arousal that
generated the impulse has usually decayed, so the decision is made by a different state of
you than the one that wanted it.

This is why a delay rule is a **passive** support in `principles.md` §2's sense: it
converts a detection problem into a logistics problem. You can hold several delay rules at
once for this reason, where you can only hold one active interception target.

## Setting the interval

- Too short and the arousal is still live, so the rule changes nothing.
- Too long and the rule gets broken, and a broken rule is worse than no rule — it teaches
  that the rules are negotiable under pressure.
- Pick the shortest interval you will actually honor every time, and lengthen it only after
  it has survived a month unbroken.

## What to log

Only two things, in the child grove's log: **invoked** (the rule fired and you honored it)
and, at expiry, **sent as drafted / sent changed / not sent at all**. The ratio of the last
three is the useful signal. A high "not sent at all" rate is the rule earning its keep; a
high "sent as drafted" rate means the interval is too short, the cue is mis-chosen, or this
impulse did not need a rule.

Also log **broken** when it happens, without commentary. Breaks cluster around specific
conditions, and that clustering is the most actionable thing the log produces.

---

## Honest limits

- It constrains emission, not the underlying state. Someone running delay rules well can
  still be fully in the grip of the pattern — managed, not rewired. That is an early stage,
  and it is real progress, but the two should not be confused.
- It only works on impulses with a **mechanical** cue. An impulse that discharges through
  speech in a live conversation has no send button; there, the interval has to be a trained
  pause, which is an *active* target and belongs in a single-target sprint instead.
- Rules proliferate quietly. Audit them periodically and retire the ones that no longer
  fire — a rule you never invoke is clutter that makes the live ones feel optional.
